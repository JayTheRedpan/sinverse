## Overview

**Scratch** is the [toys'](toys) code — a living system of small, unobtrusive marks left where other toys will find them and no one else will look. No one knows where it began. It has no author, no founding moment, and no institution behind it; it simply grew, whispered from one toy to the next, until it became something close to universal — a shared language for a class of people who are, by design, never meant to speak to each other freely.

The giantesses know it exists. They have never taken it seriously enough to stamp it out. They call it, with the particular contempt reserved for something beneath notice, **toysign** — a pathetic little habit, evidence of a wild toy's presence and nothing more, useful to them only as a thing to punish and a thing to track by.

## How it spreads

Scratch has no school and no teacher. It passes from toy to toy the way a rumor does — one who knows a little shows another, who half-remembers it and adds a mark of his own, who teaches a third with his own small mistakes baked in. It is less a language than an enormous, ongoing game of telephone, carried on for as long as there have been toys to play it.

The one real exception is the [Resistance](the-resistance), which teaches a **standardized version** of Scratch to those who move through its network — a common core meant to be reliable across cells and cities. Even they cannot hold the line completely. Resistance handlers are constantly finding regional drift: a mark that means one thing in one district and something subtly different two districts over, new symbols nobody remembers agreeing on, old ones that quietly fell out of use. Scratch resists standing still. It is a code that is always, faintly, becoming a different code.

## What it says

Scratch is built for exactly the kind of information that keeps a toy alive one more day: what is nearby, who is nearby, and what to do about it. A working vocabulary, drawn from marks in wide use, includes things like:

- **Warnings** — *run immediately*, *traps ahead*, *sadistic, avoid*, *traitor*, *feral area*, *macro area*, *snarebloom ahead*, *grazers nearby*, *old ruins unsafe*.
- **Safer ground** — *road clear*, *hideout*, *supply cache*, *old ruins safe*, *tempered here*.
- **Reading a giantess** — *respects collars*, *kind but needy*, *will help for pleasure*, *easily robbed*, *be boring* (advice as much as information: the surest way through some encounters is to be forgettable).
- **Territory** — *sylph lives here*, *the Pack territory*.

No single toy knows the whole vocabulary, and no two toys' Scratch is quite identical. What every dialect shares is the same underlying purpose: turning one toy's hard-won knowledge of a street, a district, or a giantess into something the next toy through doesn't have to learn the hard way.

## How it's made and hidden

Scratch is built around a single design constraint: **legible to a toy, invisible to a giantess.** It is placed **low** — at a toy's eye level or below, along baseboards, under sills, at the base of walls and posts, in the kind of places a much larger set of eyes simply never has reason to look down at. The marks themselves are kept **small**, often no larger than a scratch, a chip, a smudge, or a few scored lines — easy to mistake for wear, damage, or nothing at all, right up until another toy stops and reads it.

Beyond that, placement is opportunistic and endlessly inventive. A toy marking Scratch has to work with whatever will hold a mark and escape a glance — a scuff in old paint, a scratch in stone, a chalk line in a gutter, a scrap tucked where only something toy-sized would think to check. There is no fixed alphabet of *surfaces*, only the fixed rule of staying low, staying small, and staying beneath a giantess's line of sight.

## What it means to a giantess

To the women who rule the cities, Scratch barely rises to the level of a threat. It is filed alongside vermin and graffiti — beneath contempt, beneath real concern, the kind of thing that provokes an eye-roll rather than alarm. When a giantess does notice a mark, it is usually read as one more small, pathetic act of defiance from something that was never going to matter anyway.

That contempt has two edges to it, though. A toy caught making Scratch is reliably punished — cheaply, casually, as an object lesson rather than a serious response. And a wall of it is treated as useful **evidence**: a cluster of Scratch is a sign that wild or resistant toys have passed through or nearby, and the [Paws](the-paws) and others who hunt them read a heavy patch of marks the way a tracker reads a trail. The very thing the giantesses dismiss as harmless is, in its own quiet way, taken seriously enough to hunt by.

## What it means to a toy

For a toy, Scratch is something closer to a lifeline than a language. It is the accumulated, anonymous knowledge of every toy who came through before him, left behind in a form small enough to survive and simple enough to trust. A single mark can be the difference between walking into a trap and walking around it, between a district that will rob him blind and one where a mark promises, however faintly, that someone kind is nearby.

It is also, in its own understated way, a form of solidarity the giantesses have no name for. Every mark is proof that someone else was here, thought of the next toy, and left something behind for him. In a world engineered to keep toys isolated from each other, Scratch is the thing that keeps happening anyway.

## Did You Know?

- No one knows who started Scratch or when — it has no founder, only a slow, anonymous spread from toy to toy.
- The [Resistance](the-resistance) teaches a standardized version, but even their handlers are constantly chasing regional drift and new, unofficial symbols.
- Giantesses call it **"toysign"** — a dismissive name for something they consider beneath real concern.
- Scratch is kept low and small on purpose: legible at a toy's eye level, invisible or meaningless to anyone standing at a giantess's height.
- A toy caught making Scratch is punished, and a cluster of marks is read by hunters like the [Paws](the-paws) as a sign of wild toys nearby — the giantesses' contempt for it doesn't stop them from tracking by it.
- Its vocabulary covers everything from warnings (*traps ahead*, *feral area*, *snarebloom ahead*) to character reads on a giantess (*respects collars*, *kind but needy*, *be boring*) to safe ground (*hideout*, *supply cache*, *tempered here*).
